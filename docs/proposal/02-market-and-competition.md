# Market and Competition

## The Apparent Field

There is no shortage of textbooks on formal logic, non-classical logics, category theory,
model theory, or foundations of mathematics. Any research library holds shelves of such
works, and the past three decades have produced competent graduate-level introductions to
most of the individual systems this book addresses: substructural logics (Restall),
non-classical logics (Priest), paraconsistency (da Costa, Priest, Béziau), linear logic
(Troelstra, Girard), intuitionistic logic (van Dalen, Troelstra), quantum logic (Dalla
Chiara, Giuntini), and category-theoretic foundations (Awodey, Johnstone). These are
serious works. They are also not this work.

## The Genuine Gap

The author's library is built from a systematic search for a pre-existing solution to a
specific problem of articulation. That library includes *From Kant to Hilbert* (Ewald),
*From Frege to Gödel* (van Heijenoort), *The Undecidable* (Davis), *Model Theory*
(Chang and Keisler), *Undecidable Theories* (Tarski, Mostowski, and Robinson),
*Conjectures and Refutations* (Popper), and much more in the standard reference canon of
formal logic and foundations. If a work existed that occupied the intersection this book
addresses, it would be on those shelves. It is not. The gap is not a marketing claim; it
is the reason the book has to be written.

What is absent from the existing literature is a unified framework that treats the
**logical relation itself as a parameter** across the category of logics compatible with
classical first-order predicate logic — logics that stand in well-defined relationships
to classical logic as extensions, duals, meets, or joins in the logical and categorical
space of formal systems. Within that category, interpretability, irrefutability, and provability have precise
dual counterparts: non-interpretability, unprovability, and refutability. Working out
these dual relationships gives the framework indirect reach toward theories and logics
that are *not* compatible with classical logic, without requiring that the incompatible
cases be treated directly. The result is a relatively general method for translating
between compatible first-order theories of all logics in this space — and, equally
importantly, a principled account of when translation fails.

The concept organizing this reach is **categorizability**. The framework establishes the
logical and geometric space of categorizable logics — those that can be fully specified
within the variable-consequence architecture. Non-categorizable logics, including those
that resist classical and non-classical formalization alike, become coherently speakable
only once the categorizable space has been established. Without the framework, researchers
cannot determine whether a given inter-theoretic incompatibility is a within-framework
disagreement resolvable by standard means, a between-framework incomparability requiring
the variable-consequence apparatus, or a genuinely non-categorizable phenomenon outside
the scope of any current formalism. The framework makes that distinction tractable.

No existing work provides this. The surveys of non-classical logic treat individual systems
in isolation or in pairwise comparison; they do not construct the unified architecture.
The category-theoretic treatments of logic (Lawvere, Johnstone) address the categorical
structure of consistent logical systems but not the specific problem of variable consequence and its
applications to cross-disciplinary formal reasoning. *Undecidable Theories*, the closest
foundational antecedent, establishes the complete picture for the classical case; it does
not ask how that picture changes when the underlying logic is varied, because it takes the
classical framework as fixed.

## Intended Readership

The intended readership is the community of theorists working on the hardest open problems
across the natural sciences, formal sciences, and their applied intersections — researchers
whose work is currently represented by the member organizations of the International
Science Council and its constituent bodies. This includes theorists in computing,
mathematics, physics, astronomy, biology, chemistry, economics, law, scientific medicine,
diplomacy, networking, and related fields who face, in their specific domains, the same
structural problem: incompatibilities between formal frameworks that cannot be diagnosed or
resolved within any single one of those frameworks.

These readers do not need an introduction to logic. They need a **common metalinguistic
framework** — a shared formal vocabulary for identifying what kind of incompatibility they
are facing, what translations are and are not available, and what the logical preconditions
are for genuine cross-disciplinary formal communication. That is what this book provides.
The metaphor is apt: not a grammar of any one language, but a rosetta stone — one that
does not merely translate but specifies when translation is possible, when it is not, and
what the structure of the impossibility is when it is not.

This readership does not require a mass-market distribution channel. It requires
appropriate indexing, institutional availability, and the credibility of rigorous
scholarly publication.
